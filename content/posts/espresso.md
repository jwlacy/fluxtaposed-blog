I used to brew beer. Now I brew coffee.

That's not a complaint. If anything it's an upgrade. Less equipment, less cleanup, and the turnaround from "I want this" to "I have this" is about four minutes instead of six weeks. Going fully remote accelerated the whole thing too. When your commute is ten steps to a home office, the morning cup stops being an afterthought. It becomes a ritual. And then the mid-morning cup becomes a ritual. And the afternoon cup. You get the point.

So I've gone pretty deep on it. Chemex, V60, obsessing over beans and grind size and water temperature like it matters. And it does, that's the thing. Once you start paying attention you can't stop. I've become that person and I've made peace with it.

But espresso always felt like a different category. Something that lived at coffee shops, behind a $3,000 machine, operated by someone with strong opinions about pressure curves. I appreciated it when it was good and didn't think too hard about it when it wasn't. It just didn't feel like something I could replicate at home without a serious investment.

Then a $50 marketplace find appeared in the form of a De'Longhi ECP3420.

The first shot was bland and kind of watery and tasted like disappointment. And my immediate fear, the one I think a lot of people have, was that this was just a problem you could only solve by throwing more money at it. That good espresso was a rich person's game and a cheap machine was never going to close that gap.

I almost talked myself into believing that.

Instead I just kept pulling shots. Adjusted the grind finer. Got an actual tamper instead of the little plastic thing that came in the box. Eventually made the move from single to double shots, which honestly felt like unlocking a different drink entirely. More grounds, more surface area, more coffee flavor actually making it into the cup.

It took repetition more than anything else. Same logic as dialing in a pour-over. You pull a shot, you taste it, you adjust one variable, you pull another. Sour means too coarse. Bitter means too fine. Weak means you're probably undertamping. The machine isn't magic, it's just pressure, and pressure rewards consistency.

And for me, consistency becomes quasi-obsession.

At some point the shots started tasting like what I'd been hoping for.

I don't know exactly when it clicked. There wasn't a dramatic moment. I just realized one morning that I'd been using it every day since it arrived, without thinking about it, without dreading it, without hedging that it was "pretty good for a home machine." It was just good. My espresso. At home.

That's the thing about espresso having this reputation for being fussy and gatekept. Some of that is real, but a lot of it is just the learning curve wearing a costume. You don't need a $3,000 machine. You need to pay attention and be willing to make bad shots until you understand why they're bad.

Turns out that's true of most things worth learning.
